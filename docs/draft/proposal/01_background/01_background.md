## Draft

Equity markets promote capital formation by pooling savings, sharing risk and
transferring wealth between those with surplus funds and those with capital
needs (Mosley & Singer, 2008). Within this market, investment in equity shares
is one of the principal avenues through which considerable returns may be earned,
while simultaneously serving as a source of finance for firms' capital
requirements. The return on such an investment is not, however, guaranteed:
it varies with the performance of the individual stock and with movements in
its price. Stock prices fluctuate in response to supply and demand pressures,
but no system exists that predicts the direction of such movement with certainty.
The forces behind changes in demand and supply are conventionally grouped into
technical factors, fundamental factors and market sentiment. Knowledge of how
these factors bear on share prices is of practical value, since it assists
investors in making informed decisions and enables firms to enhance their market
value (Bhattarai, 2014).

Security prices are shaped by company-specific and sector-specific influences
as well as by the wider economic and political environment. Movements in share
prices are seen to depend on macroeconomic variables, on domestic and
international economic, social and political events, on market expectations
regarding the future growth trajectory of the economy, and on announcements of
monetary and fiscal policy. The stock market capitalises present and future
growth opportunities as it evaluates the growth of all sectors of the economy.
For this reason the stock market is often described as the pulse of the economy,
reflecting the actions of economic and political agents with a speed that is close
to immediate. Economic theory further suggests that share prices should reflect
expectations about future corporate performance, and that corporate profits
generally reflect the level of economic activity. If share prices accurately
reflect underlying fundamentals, they can be employed as leading indicators of
future economic activity rather than the reverse. The causal relations between
macroeconomic variables and share prices therefore carry direct implications for
the formulation of national macroeconomic policy (Singh, 2010).

The history of the stock market in Nepal is comparatively short. The Securities
Exchange Centre (SEC) was established in 1976 with the objective of facilitating
and promoting the growth of the capital market (Gurung, 2004). It opened its floor
for secondary trading in 1981, initially limited to government bonds (NRB, 1996).
With the enactment of the Securities Exchange Act 1984, SEC extended its floor to
corporate share trading, though on a limited basis. The organised and fully
fledged stock market began with the conversion of SEC into Nepal Stock Exchange
(NEPSE) Limited in 1993, with floor trading commencing in 1994. NEPSE remains the
only stock exchange in the country. The market is therefore still at a developing
stage, but is of particular interest because it has grown substantially since its
establishment. It was created in order to mobilise capital as an alternative to
the traditional banking sector, in support of economic growth and development
within the country.

Commercial banks occupy a distinctive position in this market. Their shares are
traded more frequently than those of other listed companies, which makes them
the most observable segment of the Nepali stock market. The sources of funds
raised through the issuance of common shares are the principal means by which
commercial banks and financial institutions are capitalised. Common stock
represents an ownership share in a corporation and constitutes the recipient of
its residual income; common stockholders hold voting rights and legal control.
The returns on an investment in common stock arise from two sources, namely
periodic dividend receipts and capital gains (Thapa & Rana, 2013). Bank shares
also differ from those of non-financial firms in that their valuation is driven
principally by the profitability, asset quality and regulatory position of the
issuing institution rather than by growth expectations.

Against this background, the factors that determine the market price of bank
shares have attracted sustained attention in the Nepali literature. Bhattarai
(2016) examined the effects of dividend payout ratio, dividend yield, earnings
per share, price-earnings ratio and size on the share price of banks listed on
NEPSE, and concluded that earnings per share and the price-earnings ratio are
significantly and positively associated with share price, while dividend yield
is inversely associated with it. Pradhan (2016) extended this line by
incorporating macroeconomic variables alongside bank-specific ones, and found
the effects of earnings per share, price-earnings ratio, book value per share
and return on assets on market price per share to be weak. The absence of
consensus across these studies, arising from differences in sample,
period and estimation method, motivates the present investigation.
