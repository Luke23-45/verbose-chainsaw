## Draft

A substantial body of research has examined the determinants of share prices.
Researchers have advanced competing explanations spanning two broad families. The
first attributes share price movements to firm-specific or fundamental factors,
treating the market price of a share as a function of the firm's earnings,
dividend distribution, book value and asset efficiency. The second attributes
them to macroeconomic and market-wide factors, treating the price as a function
of interest rates, inflation, money supply, output and sentiment. A third line
of work combines the two, or examines the relative explanatory power of each.

Despite this volume of work, there is no consensus on the results. Empirical
findings across studies are frequently mixed and inconsistent with one another.
The divergence is not surprising in retrospect, since prior studies differ in
sample composition, sector, observation period, variable definition and
estimation technique, each of which conditions the outcome.

Among the Nepali literature, Pradhan (2016) examined both microeconomic and
macroeconomic influences on the share prices of Nepali commercial banks and
concluded that an investor should weigh bank-specific and macroeconomic variables
together when making investment decisions. Bhattarai (2016), by contrast, found
positive relations between earnings, dividend payout ratio, earnings per share
and the price-earnings ratio. Lama (2016) reported positive relationships of
market price per share with size, earnings per share, dividend per share, return
on assets, money supply, inflation and gross domestic product. Pradhan (2017),
working with fundamental factors alone, found dividend per share, return on
assets and earnings per share positively related to stock price while net profit
margin was negatively related.

The inconsistency is itself the substantive problem. It means that neither
investors nor policymakers can rely on the existing evidence to identify which
bank-specific factors bear on share prices in the Nepali context, and it means
that a researcher's operational definition of a "determinant" may be closer to
an artefact of sample and period choice than to a durable economic relationship.

This study addresses this problem by re-examining bank-specific determinants of
market price per share for a sample of Nepali commercial banks using a
contemporary observation period. The earlier work on this topic drew on data
from a period ending several years ago and predates the merger activity and
regulatory change that have since altered the structure of the banking sector.
Because those changes affect bank-level profitability, equity and share
liquidity, the earlier estimates may no longer characterise the market as it now
stands. Re-testing the same set of firm-specific determinants against updated
data therefore serves to establish whether the relationships reported previously
continue to hold, and in what direction.

The study seeks to answer the following questions:

i. Is there any relationship between EPS, DPS, P/E ratio, BVPS, ROA and MPS?
ii. Do EPS, DPS, P/E ratio, BVPS and ROA affect the MPS of the sampled banks?
