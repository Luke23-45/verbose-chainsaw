## Draft

### Conceptual review

**Market price per share (MPS).** MPS is the price at which a share sells on the
open market at a given point in time. It fluctuates through the trading day as
buying and selling pressure shifts, rising when demand exceeds supply and
falling when the reverse occurs (Pradhan, 2016). A company's total market value,
its market capitalisation, is its share price multiplied by the number of shares
outstanding. Share prices are affected by market volatility, prevailing economic
conditions and the popularity of the company (Thapa & Rana, 2013).

**Earnings per share (EPS).** EPS is the portion of a company's profit allocated
to each outstanding share of common stock, and serves as an indicator of
profitability. It represents a company's capacity to generate profit per share
held. Increasing earnings per share generally indicate company growth and a
correspondingly high market price. Dhungel (2014) concluded that EPS has a
positive relationship with the share prices of Nepali commercial banks, and
Uddin and Rahman (2013) revealed a positive linear relationship between EPS and
market price.

**Dividend per share (DPS).** DPS is the total dividend paid over a year divided
by the number of ordinary shares outstanding. It represents the portion of
profit after tax distributed to shareholders for bearing the risk of investment
in the company (Geetha and Swaminathan, 2015). It indicates the profitability
aspect of the company, sets a benchmark for meaningful comparison across
companies, and signals firm growth. Prabath (2014) concluded that DPS has a
positive and significant impact on share price.

**Price-earnings ratio (P/E).** The P/E ratio measures a company's current share
price relative to its per-share earnings, and helps an investor assess the market
value of a stock relative to the company's earnings. A high P/E may indicate a
share price high relative to earnings and possible overvaluation; a low P/E may
indicate the opposite. Almumani (2014) found a significant positive association
between P/E and firm share price, whereas Nicholson (1960) found that stocks
with low P/E ratios delivered better investment performance than high P/E
stocks. Geetha and Swaminathan (2015) note that the ratio enables an investor to
estimate the time required to recover an investment.

**Book value per share (BVPS).** BVPS is a per-share estimate of the minimum
value of an entity's equity, determined by relating the original value of common
stock, adjusted for outflows such as dividends and buybacks and inflows such as
retained earnings, to the number of shares outstanding. It is an indicator of
how a stock is valued, and if BVPS exceeds market price per share the stock may
be deemed undervalued. Prabath (2014) concluded that BVPS has a positive and
significant impact on share price, and Stephen and Okoro (2014) found a positive
impact on the determination of stock prices.

**Return on assets (ROA).** ROA is a profitability ratio measuring how much
profit a company generates from its assets, demonstrating how effectively
management converts economic resources into earnings. Ramzan (2013) found ROA to
have an insignificant relationship with share price, whereas Pradhan (2017) found
ROA to be positively related to share price.

### Empirical review

Sharma (2011) examined the empirical relationship between equity share prices
and explanatory variables including book value per share, dividend per share,
earnings per share, price-earnings ratio, dividend yield, dividend payout, size
in terms of sales and net worth over 1993-94 to 2008-09, and found that earnings
per share, dividend per share and book value per share had a significant impact
on market price, with the combination of dividend per share and earnings per
share being the strongest determinants.

Aurangzeb (2012) examined macroeconomic variables and the stock index across
Pakistan, India and Sri Lanka, and found that foreign direct investment and
exchange rate had a significant positive impact on stock market performance, that
interest rate had a negative and significant impact, and that inflation had a
negative but insignificant impact.

Malhotra and Tandon (2013) determined the factors influencing stock prices for 95
companies drawn from the NSE 100 over 2007 to 2012 using linear regression, and
found book value, earnings per share and the price-earnings ratio to be
significantly and positively associated with firm share price, while dividend
yield was inversely associated with it.

Bhattarai (2014) examined the determinants of share price of commercial banks
listed on NEPSE over 2006 to 2014 using annual reports and regression, and found
earnings per share and the price-earnings ratio significantly positively
associated with share price, while dividend yield was significantly inversely
associated.

Almumani (2014) identified the quantitative factors influencing share prices for
banks listed on the Amman Stock Exchange over 2005 to 2011 using linear multiple
regression, finding significant positive relationships between EPS, book value
and P/E and market price, and an inverse relationship between size and market
price.

Lama (2016) examined the effect of firm-specific and macroeconomic variables on
share price of Nepali commercial banks using 126 observations from 18 commercial
banks, and reported positive relationships of market price per share with size,
earnings per share, dividend per share, return on assets, money supply, inflation
and gross domestic product.

Pradhan (2017) pooled fundamental factors for 13 Nepali commercial banks over
2007 to 2014, giving 104 observations, and found dividend per share, return on
assets and earnings per share positively related to stock price, while net
profit margin was negatively related.

### Research gap

The studies reviewed differ in the variables selected, the sampling frame, the
observation period and the estimation technique, and their findings are
inconsistent as a result. Some examine bank-specific factors alone, some
macroeconomic factors alone, and some both together. Some report EPS, DPS and
BVPS as significant determinants of market price, while Pradhan (2016) reports
the effects of these same variables to be weak, and Ramzan (2013) finds ROA
insignificant where Pradhan (2017) finds it significant.

Three specific gaps follow from this. First, the most recent Nepali study on
bank-specific determinants of share price draws on an observation period that
ended several years ago, predating merger activity and regulatory change that
have since altered which entities are listed and how bank capital is
structured; the estimates may therefore no longer describe the sector.
Second, prior work has not consistently separated the specification problem
raised by including the price-earnings ratio alongside earnings per share in the
same model, given that the former is defined as the ratio of market price to
earnings. Third, differences in period and sample have not been examined as the
source of the reported inconsistency, so it remains unclear whether the
contradictions are substantive or a consequence of specification choice.

This study addresses the first gap directly by re-estimating the bank-specific
determinants against a contemporary sample, addresses the second by stating the
treatment of the price-earnings ratio explicitly, and contributes to the third
by reporting results under a common specification across all sampled banks.
