# 02 — Claims, hedging, and academic style

The standard: every claim must be **proportionate to the evidence
available** (CityU HK ELSS; Oprit-Maftei 2016). An examiner's fastest way
to distrust a thesis is one claim that outruns its evidence. This file
defines the claim-discipline rules for THIS thesis, with its own text as
the worked examples.

---

## 1. The claim ladder (use the lowest rung the evidence supports)

| Rung | Verb pattern | Requires |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Established fact | "is", "mandates" (statute), "equals" (identity) | Statute, arithmetic, or the study's own verified panel |
| 2. Study finding | "X reported / documented / found" | The cited study |
| 3. Association in this sample | "is associated with", "coincided with", "is consistent with" | This thesis's estimates |
| 4. Suggested mechanism | "plausible mechanism", "may reflect", "suggestive of" | Theory + consistency, no estimate |
| 5. Recommendation | "should consider / operationalize" | Chapter V only |

Rules enforced in this thesis:

- Results language stays on rung 3. The governing sentence is §4.3.2's
  opening ("interpreted not as proven causal effects, but as suggestive
  structural tendencies") — it is the template for every mechanism
  sentence. §4.3.2's mechanism 1 is the model to copy: mechanism named,
  "Plausible Mechanism" label, hedged verb, cited context, and the
  closing sentence "This mechanism is consistent with the observed drop
  ... though direct asset quality variables are not estimated."
- No claim may be strengthened between chapters (the Chapter V
  "verified parameter stability" violation was the historical case).
  Rule: **a later chapter may only restate or weaken, never upgrade.**
- Institutional claims outside the study's own data are either cited or
  tied to the study's observation: the accepted hedges are "based on the
  disclosures examined for this study" (Ch. 2, EMH section) and "in
  observed corporate practice across the sampled banks" (Ch. 1 §1.5).
  Use these two idioms before asserting any Nepalese-market practice.

## 2. Hedging vocabulary (CityU HK; Oprit-Maftei 2016)

Preferred hedges, in decreasing strength:

- Verbs: appears to, seems to, suggests, indicates, is consistent with,
  coincides with, may reflect
- Adverbs: approximately, largely, typically, generally, on point
  estimates
- Scopes: within the reviewed literature, in this sample, among the
  selected banks, under this specification, at the 10% level
- Numeric hedges: "positive but statistically insignificant",
  "economically substantial but not statistically significant" (both
  already used in the Chapter I preview — keep this exact pattern for
  any new coefficient narrative)

Banned patterns (they assert certainty the design cannot deliver):

- "proves", "proven", "confirms that X causes", "demonstrates that the
  merger caused"
- "universally", "all studies", "no study ever" (use "almost all",
  "none of the studies reviewed") — the "universally" fix in Ch. 2 is
  the precedent
- "exact", "perfect", "completely" outside arithmetic identities
- Emotive intensifiers: "catastrophic", "dramatic", "monumental",
  "massive" — Ch. 1 §1.1 "monumental scale" (Ch. 5 §5.2.1) and §1.5
  "catastrophic multicollinearity" (Ch. 1 rationale point 3) are the two
  known residues; "aggressive market-wide price compression" (§4.2.1)
  is borderline — "sharp" or "substantial" is the calibrated verb
- Self-praise inside tables/notes: "the correct parameter count" was
  fixed to "the model's parameter count"; the same rule bans "rigorous",
  "excellent", "state-of-the-art" in the thesis's own voice

## 3. Tense and voice conventions

- **Past tense for what was done and found**: "prices were observed",
  "the study estimated", "Kwon and Wang documented".
- **Present tense for what the thesis and the data are**: "Table 4
  reports", "the sample consists of", "the coefficient is 2.095".
- **Present tense for standing theory and statute**: "Ohlson (1995)
  derives", "Section 59(2) mandates". (Status: already consistent
  throughout — preserve it in edits.)
- **Voice**: this thesis uses first-person-plural sparingly and
  "this dissertation/the study" elsewhere — keep that convention; do
  not introduce "I" or "we" where "the study" is running.
- **Agentful sentences for choices, agentless for arithmetic**: "the
  study enforces a strict five-day freshness rule" (choice) vs "two
  observations are coded as missing" (protocol).

## 4. Paragraph discipline (Harvard: topic sentence + evidence + analysis)

- One claim per paragraph; the first sentence states it; the middle
  supplies evidence (estimate, citation, or statute); the last sentence
  says what it means for THIS study. The profile paragraphs in §2.4 and
  the numbered implications in §5.4 are the in-thesis examples.
- Literature paragraphs carry at least two studies per claim where the
  claim is about a debate (Editage rule). The four thematic debates in
  §2.4.5 all do this; single-study paragraphs are reserved for study
  profiles.
- Every list item in Results/Discussion opens with a bolded run-in
  label followed by a sentence that could stand alone — the existing
  house style. Keep it.
- Signposting: each section opens with one sentence stating what the
  section does ("This section evaluates...", "The empirical estimates
  in Table 6 provide..."). Already used throughout; preserve.

## 5. Word-level hygiene (grep before every submission pass)

- utilize* → use (0 hits currently; keep at 0)
- pivotal, crucial role, underscores (0 hits; keep at 0)
- universally, all studies, no study ever (0 hits after the Ch. 2 fix;
  keep at 0)
- "in order to" → "to" (not yet swept; low priority)
- Double hedges ("may possibly suggest") — never use two hedges on one
  verb; one is discipline, two is noise
- "very", "highly", "extremely" outside quoted material — sweep on the
  final pass; "extraordinarily weak linear correlation" (§4.2.2 point
